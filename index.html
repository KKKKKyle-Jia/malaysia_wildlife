Malaysia's Wild
Heritage at Risk

Malaysia is one of just 17 “megadiverse” nations on Earth — home to tigers, orangutans and rainforests older than the Himalayas. Yet in a single generation, much of that natural wealth has been logged, burned and pushed toward extinction. Here is the story, told in twelve charts.

01 — The setting

A megadiverse nation

Straddling the equator, Malaysia's rainforests have been evolving for some 130 million years — older than the Amazon. That deep history has produced staggering variety: hundreds of mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians and fish, alongside an estimated 15,000 plant species. The country ranks 12th in the world on the National Biodiversity Index. To understand what is being lost, first look at how much there is.

🌿
02 — The habitat

The vanishing forests

At the 1992 Rio Earth Summit, Malaysia pledged to keep at least 50% of its land under forest. But satellites tell a sobering story. Between 2001 and 2020 the country lost 8.4 million hectares of tree cover — about 28.5% of what stood in the year 2000. The map below colours each state by the share of its forest that disappeared. The surprise: the heaviest proportional losses are not in the vast forests of Borneo, but in the small, developed states of the west coast.

Loss was not steady. It surged with palm-oil and timber expansion, peaking in 2014 when Malaysia cleared nearly 646,000 hectares in a single year. Primary (old-growth) forest — the irreplaceable kind — made up roughly a third of that loss.

Two states on the island of Borneo — Sarawak and Sabah — account for well over half of everything Malaysia has lost.

🌿
03 — The casualties

Species on the brink

When forests fall, their inhabitants follow. No animal tells this story more starkly than the Malayan tiger — the creature on Malaysia's own coat of arms. In the 1950s, around 3,000 roamed the peninsula. Today, after decades of habitat loss and poaching, fewer than 150 survive in the wild.

It is hard to picture how few that is. So here is every one of them — each tiger below stands for one wild Malayan tiger left alive.

The tiger is not alone. As of 2024 the IUCN Red List classes 761 of Malaysia's animal species as threatened — vulnerable, endangered or critically endangered — one of the highest tallies in Asia.

🌿
04 — The defence

The last refuges

Against this tide stand Malaysia's protected areas — a network of national and state parks where wildlife can, in principle, endure. They range from the 4,343 km² expanse of Taman Negara, one of the world's oldest rainforests, to UNESCO World Heritage sites like Kinabalu Park and Gunung Mulu.

🌿
05 — The other threat

Sold by the million

Habitat loss is only half the danger. Southeast Asia is a global hub for the illegal wildlife trade, and Malaysia sits at its centre. Over just ten years (1998–2007), the region exported tens of millions of wild animals — the vast majority taken straight from the forest.

Malaysia's wild heritage took 130 million years to build. Most of what we have lost vanished in a single human lifetime — and what remains is still being decided now.

About this visualisation

An interactive data story combining two decades of satellite forest-loss data with conservation records, to show how Malaysia's extraordinary biodiversity is being eroded — and where it still survives.

Data sources

  • Forest & tree-cover loss — Hansen/UMD/Google/USGS/NASA, accessed via Global Forest Watch and Mongabay / worldrainforests.com. 30% canopy-cover threshold.
  • Threatened species — IUCN Red List of Threatened Species (2024); national figures compiled via Statista.
  • Species richness — Malaysia Biodiversity Information System (MyBIS); 5th National Report to the Convention on Biological Diversity.
  • Tiger & populations — WWF-Malaysia; 2020 National Tiger Survey (DWNP & partners).
  • Protected areas — Dept. of Wildlife & National Parks (PERHILITAN), Sabah Parks, Sarawak Forestry Corporation.
  • Wildlife trade — TRAFFIC, compiled via malaysianwildlife.org.
  • State boundaries — GADM / click_that_hood (GeoJSON).

About the data

“Tree-cover loss” counts the removal of tree canopy taller than five metres from both natural forest and plantations; it is not identical to permanent deforestation. “Primary forest” refers to undisturbed old-growth. Figures combine two primary sources — Global Forest Watch (forests) and the IUCN Red List (species) — with supplementary government and NGO records.

Chart specifications

All charts are built in Vega-Lite. The human-readable JSON specification for every chart is available in the /specs folder of this repository, and the underlying data in /data.
